A video can look real.
It can still be false.

AI can now write the story, draw the photograph and make the video. It takes a minute, and it takes no skill. This site explains what that means for older readers — how the fake news reaches them, what it costs them, and what can actually be done.

Research question

How does AI help create and spread fake news on social media, what impacts does it have on older adults, and what challenges do they face?

01 · The problem

Fake news is old. Cheap, instant fakes are new.

In this project, “fake news” means AI-generated false text, images, videos and deepfakes that are presented as real news and spread on social media. [1] [2]

Before generative AI, a convincing fake video took a team, a budget and weeks. Now one person can type a single sentence and get a finished story, a photograph of something that never happened, or a video of a real person saying words they never said. The tools are free or cheap, and they need no training to use.

Older adults are not passive victims in this story. They use social media for news and to stay close to family, and that is exactly the space where fake news spreads. Many cannot tell that a machine made the content, so the false claim arrives looking like a message from someone they trust — and the cost can be a bad health decision or lost savings.

The people with the least digital skill carry the most risk. That imbalance is what this project is about.

02 · AI's role

Two jobs, working together

AI does not just make the fake. It also decides who sees it. Both halves matter, and neither works as well alone.

Job one — making the content

Generative AI writes fake articles and posts from a single short instruction. It makes photographs of events that never happened, and deepfake tools swap faces and clone voices so a real person appears to say anything. No special skills are needed, and the output looks real enough to pass. [4] [1]

Job two — choosing who sees it

Platform algorithms rank content by how strongly people react to it. Fear, anger and surprise travel furthest, and fake news is built to trigger them, so the systems hand it to more people. Repeat exposure builds an echo chamber, where a false claim starts to feel familiar — and familiar starts to feel true. [5]

Three challenges older adults face

1. Telling it apart

Spotting a fake used to mean spotting a clumsy sentence or a strange photograph. Generated text and images no longer give those clues, and confidence drops with it. [3]

2. The feed learns

One tap on a false health post is a signal. The system answers it with more of the same, so a mistaken belief is not corrected over time — it is reinforced. [5]

3. Nobody to ask

Checking a claim takes a second opinion, and many older readers live alone or read at night when no family member is awake. The cost of asking feels higher than the cost of believing. [8]

04 · Benefits

AI is also part of the answer

Detection and prebunking do not remove the risk. They change who has to be careful. Here is what genuinely works — and where each tool stops.

Models that flag suspicious content

Detection systems read text, images and video and score how likely it is that a machine made them. The stronger versions explain why: an audio track that does not match the mouth, wrong shadows or an unsupported dramatic claim. [6]

Prebunking, before the belief forms

Prebunking warns people about misinformation before it spreads. UNDP's Bangladesh Accelerator Lab is developing an AI-powered tool that gives young people tailored information to share with older relatives. [7] [15]

One person who can check for them

For an older reader, the fastest detector is still a human being. A reverse image search, the original publisher or one phone call settles most claims. [8]

Where these stop

Detection runs slower than generation and produces false alarms. Prebunking works in trials but is less tested at the scale of millions of users. These tools support digital literacy and oversight — they do not replace them. [6] [8]

05 · Risks

Four ways this hurts older people

The harm is not only “believing something wrong”. It affects health, money, family and reputation.

1. Believing it

In the IEEE study of 1,804 people, older participants were more likely to accept fabricated events as real and less confident in identifying AI-made content. [3]

2. Health

False cures and invented warnings may delay real medical help. [10]

3. Money

Cloned voices and forged faces make financial fraud cheaper and more convincing. Older adults are a high-risk group. [9] [14]

4. Standing among family and friends

Sharing a fake turns the reader into the source. A later correction can be embarrassing and reduce trust. [10]

“I'm a bit suspicious of people now.

An older woman in Taiwan, after learning the videos she forwarded were AI-made. [10]
06 · Impact on society

It does not stay in one phone

A false post is a private mistake that becomes a public cost.

In families

AI videos travel through family group chats. When exposed, the loss is not just accuracy — it is trust in the person who shared them. [10]

In business

An AI-forged video pushed a beverage company's market value down more than 7 billion yuan in a week. [10]

In education

71% of UK students say real and fake are getting harder to separate. This affects everyone, not only older people. [10]

In communities

Researchers in Taiwan tracked more than 70,000 AI-made videos aimed at older viewers. [10]

07 · Ethics & safety

Who carries the risk, and who carries the cost

The people with the least protection can carry the most damage, while platforms carry less of the direct consequence.

Privacy and consent

Deepfake avatars and “AI doctors” can use real or synthesised faces without permission. [11]

Misinformation

Formal language and a fake authority are enough to convince readers who do not have practice checking sources. [1]

Bias in the machine

Algorithms rank emotional content, especially fear. One reaction can lead to a stream of similar claims. [5] [12]

Security

Deepfakes make fraud and identity theft cheaper. Older adults are often targeted. [14] [9]

Responsible use of labels

A platform label is not effective if it is too small or unnoticed. Oversight and digital literacy are necessary. [13] [3]

08 · Solutions

Four recommendations, each aimed at a specific failure

Each recommendation responds to a challenge measured in the research.

1

Make the AI label impossible to miss

What to doUse full-width, high-contrast warnings and a confirmation step before sharing AI-made content.
2

Reverse mentoring: the grandchild as fact-checker

What to doSchools and community centres pair young people with older relatives or neighbours to practise checking claims together.
3

Fund prebunking instead of only debunking

What to doGovernments and NGOs should warn older users about likely false stories before they arrive.
4

Hold platforms responsible for deepfake fraud

What to doRegulators should require platforms to flag deepfake financial and health content and fine them when they fail to act.
