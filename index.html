A video can look real.

With a short prompt, AI can help produce a story, image or video in minutes. This site explains what that can mean for older readers — how false claims reach them, what harm may follow, and what people can do.

Research question

How does AI help create and spread fake news on social media, what impacts does it have on older adults, and what challenges do they face?

01 · The problem

Fake news is old. Cheap, instant fakes are new.

In this project, “fake news” means false or misleading claims presented as real news. AI can help create text, images, audio or video that supports those claims. [1] [4]

Generative tools lower some barriers to producing convincing text, images and cloned voices. The FBI describes these tools being used in impersonation, phishing and financial-fraud schemes; the exact cost and quality still vary by tool and use. [9]

Older adults are not a single vulnerable group. They use online services for information and connection, and may encounter false health or financial claims in feeds and family chats. The risks include acting on a false health claim, losing money, or feeling embarrassed after sharing something untrue. [10] [14]

The issue is unequal exposure and support, not an assumption that age alone makes someone gullible. People need clear ways to pause, check and ask for help.

02 · AI's role

Two jobs, working together

Generative AI can make content; separate platform recommendation systems influence what appears in a feed. The two can interact, but they are not the same technology. [5]

Making the content

Generative tools can draft false posts, create fabricated images and imitate voices. That can reduce the time and effort required for some scams, although not every fake is AI-generated and not every output is convincing. [4] [9]

Shaping what people see

Recommendation systems use signals such as viewing and interaction history to rank content. Repeated exposure to similar claims can narrow what a person sees, but a single tap does not prove belief or guarantee an “echo chamber.” The European Commission describes recommender-system risks and user rights under the Digital Services Act. [5]

Three challenges older adults face

1. Knowing what to trust

Realistic content can be hard to judge from appearance alone. Research with older adults shows that media-literacy training can improve fake-news discernment; it does not show that every older adult has the same skills or needs. [16]

2. A feed that repeats

If an older reader watches or shares a health claim, the platform may use that interaction as one signal for recommendations. Repeated similar posts can make other perspectives less visible. This is a possible platform effect, not proof that one tap changes a person's belief. [5] [10]

3. Someone to check with

A person who lives alone, is online when family are unavailable, or worries about appearing uninformed may have fewer easy chances to ask for a second opinion. Family chats can offer important social connection while also carrying false claims; in some situations, someone may hesitate to question a relative’s post for fear of seeming distrustful. That is a possible social pressure, not a trait of older adults or a causal finding established by this case report. Intergenerational mentoring studies suggest that trusted support can help with digital confidence and connection, though they do not prove that it prevents fake-news sharing. [8] [10]

04 · Benefits

AI is also part of the answer

Detection and prebunking do not remove the risk. They change who has to be careful. Here is what genuinely works — and where each tool stops.

Detection as a prompt to check

Automated detectors may help prioritise suspicious material, but their scores are not proof of authorship. In one study of seven text detectors, non-native English essays were often misclassified as AI-written. That study concerned text, not image or video detectors, but it shows why people should be able to challenge a label. [6]

Prebunking, before a claim arrives

A peer-reviewed study tested short prebunking videos in six randomized studies and a YouTube field study; it found improved recognition of manipulation techniques and sharing decisions in those settings. Separately, UNDP's Bangladesh tool is still described as a prototype being developed and tested, not as a proven intervention for older adults. [17] [7] [15]

A trusted person to check with

A family member, community worker or librarian can help compare a claim with the original publisher or another reliable source. Reverse-mentoring research reports benefits for digital confidence and intergenerational connection, but does not establish that mentoring alone stops misinformation. [8] [16]

Limits and trade-offs

A detector's false alarm can make legitimate content look untrustworthy; an absent or unnoticed label can leave people without useful context. Prebunking results from one intervention do not guarantee lasting or older-adult-specific effects. Pair tools with clear explanations, human review, accessible appeals and follow-up evaluation. [6] [17] [5]

05 · Risks

Four ways this hurts older people

The harm is not only “believing something wrong”. It affects health, money, family and reputation.

1. Belief and confidence

False claims can mislead people of any age. A media-literacy intervention improved older participants’ ability to distinguish true from false headlines, which argues for useful support rather than assumptions based on age. [16]

2. Health

BBC Chinese reported on fabricated “doctor” videos promoting false health claims to older viewers in Taiwan. A viewer could delay proper care if they act on such a claim, so medical advice should be checked with a qualified health professional. [10]

3. Money

Cloned voices and forged faces make financial fraud cheaper and more convincing. Older adults are a high-risk group. [9] [14]

4. Standing among family and friends

A person who forwards a false claim can later feel embarrassed or become less sure whom to trust. BBC Chinese describes an older viewer who realised that videos she had shared were AI-made. [10]

06 · Impact on society

It does not stay in one phone

A false post is a private mistake that becomes a public cost.

In families

AI videos travel through family group chats. When exposed, the loss is not just accuracy — it is trust in the person who shared them. [10]

In business

A 2026 report said a fabricated AI video about a beverage company's founder was followed by a market-value drop of more than 7 billion yuan. It is one reported event, not a measure of typical losses. [18]

In education

A UK schools inquiry reported that 71% of surveyed students found it harder to tell what was real online. The finding suggests the challenge is not limited to older adults. [19]

In communities

Researchers in Taiwan tracked more than 70,000 AI-made videos aimed at older viewers. [10]

07 · Ethics & safety

Who carries the risk, and who carries the cost

The people with the least protection can carry the most damage, while platforms carry less of the direct consequence.

Privacy, consent and care

A report describes a family using an AI avatar of a deceased son to hide his death from his 80-year-old mother, believing it would spare her distress; another family objected to videos recreating their deceased relative. This raises a difficult trade-off between compassionate intent, the older person's right to know, and consent for a person's likeness. [11]

Misinformation

Formal language and a fake authority are enough to convince readers who do not have practice checking sources. [1]

Bias in the feed

Engagement-based recommendations can amplify content and make repeated viewpoints more visible. When an older person depends on online spaces for family contact, repeated claims may be hard to challenge without a trusted second opinion. The degree of this effect varies; age alone does not determine what a person sees or believes. [4] [5] [10]

Security

Deepfakes make fraud and identity theft cheaper. Older adults are often targeted. [14] [9]

Labels, errors and trust

A clear label can give context, but a false AI label can unfairly discredit genuine content, while a missing label can leave people uninformed. Automated scores should not be treated as proof; explanations, human review and appeal routes protect against over-removal of lawful material. [6] [5]

When support becomes paternalism

Warnings designed for older adults should be readable and useful without assuming that all older people lack skill. Restricting or hiding content to protect users can also limit access to legitimate speech and information. Involve older users in designing and evaluating safeguards. [5] [16]

08 · Solutions

Four recommendations, each aimed at a specific failure

These are proposals. The measures below describe how a school, platform or community programme could test whether each one helps.

1

Make AI labels clear and reviewable

What to doUse a readable, persistent label when provenance is known; explain what it means and provide a way to challenge an incorrect label.
Why it may helpA visible provenance cue can prompt people to pause and verify rather than treating a detector score as proof. [12]
Measure successTest label comprehension and sharing decisions with older users; report missed labels, false labels and successful appeals.
Trade-offA label may be overlooked, misapplied or used to stigmatise lawful content. Human review and appeal access take time and resources. [6] [5]
2

Offer reverse mentoring with a trusted choice of helper

What to doSchools, libraries and community centres can pair older learners with trained younger mentors or another trusted helper to practise checking claims together.
Why it may helpA small reverse-mentoring programme reported stronger digital confidence and intergenerational connection; this supports the approach, but is not proof it prevents misinformation. [8]
Measure successUse short before-and-after tasks on source checking and image search, plus participant feedback on confidence and comfort asking for help.
Trade-offNot everyone has a grandchild or wants family involved. Offer a choice of mentor, protect privacy and avoid making older adults feel tested or judged.
3

Test prebunking before scaling it

What to doAdapt short prebunking lessons to local languages and common tactics, and offer them before likely exposure to false health or financial claims.
Why it may helpA peer-reviewed field study found improved recognition of manipulation techniques and sharing decisions in its tested settings. [17]
Measure successMeasure recognition of tactics, discernment of true and false claims, sharing choices and retention after several weeks, including results for older participants.
Trade-offThe effects may fade, and trial results may not transfer to every age group, language or local context. The Bangladesh AI tool is still a project in development, not a proven outcome. [7] [15]
4

Require proportionate platform accountability

What to doRegulators should require transparent reporting on how platforms label and handle high-risk impersonation and fraud reports, with accessible explanations and appeals. Use proportionate enforcement for repeated failures.
Why it may helpReporting and redress make errors visible and give affected people a route to challenge platform decisions. The EU Digital Services Act provides one example of transparency and complaint rights. [5]
Measure successTrack time to review reports, confirmed harmful impersonations, false removals, appeal outcomes and repeated scam accounts.
Trade-offRules that are too broad can remove lawful content or burden smaller services. Require clear definitions, independent review and public reporting on errors.

09 · Research log

How this project was built

A dated timeline of the project’s research and development. Source checks completed during final review are identified in the relevant cards.

13 Oct

Started with the mechanism

Investigated how generative AI can produce fake text, images and videos, and how recommendation systems may amplify emotionally engaging content, helping misinformation travel further.

Sources: IEEE Computer Society [1]; EurekAlert [2] (article details still need checking).

19 Oct

Found the age evidence

The original notes recorded an IEEE study of 1,804 participants on digital literacy and susceptibility among older adults. The supplied IEEE record does not match that description, so the participant count and finding remain unverified and are not presented as established evidence.

Sources: IEEE Xplore record supplied in the log [3] (mismatch); ACM Web Conference paper [20].

21 Oct

Narrowed the audience

Focused the project on older adults and compared questions about age, digital experience and confidence when checking online claims. The available sources provide context, but do not establish a single digital-literacy profile for all older adults.

Sources: Vietnam.vn [4]; ACM Web Conference paper [20]; IEEE link in the original notes [3] (study identity unverified).

26 Oct

Tested the counter-arguments

Reviewed text-detection false positives, UNDP prebunking work and its evaluation limits, alongside high-risk voice-cloning fraud. The Bangladesh prebunking tool is described as in development; the Senate transcript documents a separate reported fraud case.

Sources: Patterns detector study [6]; UNDP [7]; ITU [15]; U.S. Senate transcript [14].

2 Nov

Collected impact numbers

Compared reported examples of AI-video reach in Taiwan, a market-value fall after a fabricated video, and survey evidence from students. The BBC account also describes an older viewer’s experience; it illustrates possible effects on trust but does not measure intergenerational trust across a population.

Sources: BBC Chinese [10]; Caixin Global [18]; UK Parliament evidence [19]; CHEQ 2019 modelled estimate [22].

17 Nov

Wrote the recommendations

Finalised four targeted proposals: prominent and reviewable platform labels, reverse mentoring, scalable prebunking, and proportionate platform accountability. Each recommendation includes a way to measure results and a trade-off to monitor.

Sources: European Commission guidance [5]; Reverse mentoring evaluation [8]; Prebunking research [17]; ITU project record [15].

18 Nov

Built the final product

Completed an interactive web application with theme and text-size controls, a four-question verification checklist, research evidence and a structured source library.

Sources: Project files and the research sources recorded above.
